## Abstract 

Trajectory prediction models in real-world autonomous driving often rely on online High-Definition (HD) maps to understand road environments, but online HD maps suffer from perception errors and feature redundancy, which hin-der the performance of these models. To address this is-sue, we introduce a framework, termed SD map-Augmented 

Trajectory Prediction (SATP), which leverages Standard-Definition (SD) maps to enhance HD map-based trajectory prediction models. First, we propose an SD-HD fusion ap-proach to leverage SD maps across the diverse range of HD map-based trajectory prediction models. Second, we de-sign a novel AlignNet to align the SD map with the HD map, further improving the effectiveness of SD maps. Ex-periments on real-world autonomous driving benchmarks demonstrate that SATP not only improves the performance of HD map-based trajectory prediction up to 25% in real-world scenarios using online HD maps but also brings ben-efits in ideal scenarios with ground-truth HD maps. 

## 1. Introduction 

Trajectory prediction is a fundamental and critical task in autonomous driving, and it aims to predict the future trajec-tories of traffic participants (hereafter referred to as agents). To make more precise predictions, it is essential to accu-rately understand the road environment. HD maps serve as a crucial input for the accurate under-standing of the road environment in recent trajectory pre-diction research [2, 5, 7, 14, 20, 27, 28, 38, 39], albeit with high associated construction and maintenance costs. There-fore, most deployed autonomous systems [11, 12, 26] use online HD maps instead. Online HD maps are constructed in real-time from inexpensive onboard sensor data [4, 15– 17, 32, 34, 40], such as RGB images, making low-cost HD maps available at any location on the earth. Specifically, we develop an SD-HD fusion approach by abstracting the HD map utilization process of different base-line models. HD map-based trajectory prediction models typically contain HD map encoding and HD feature ex-traction, and these two steps incorporate HD information into the features used for regressing trajectory coordinates. These features have different names across various baseline models, such as agent tokens [28, 38], goal queries [7, 36], or mode queries [25, 39], which we collectively refer to as “queries” for simplicity. Considering the compact feature of SD maps and feature consistency between SD and HD maps, we scale down the HD map encoding and feature ex-traction modules in baseline models to create SD map en-coding and feature extraction modules. Instead, we rely on the final trajectory prediction loss to allow it to learn the relative pose adaptively. ## 2. Related Work 

2.1. HD Map-Based Trajectory Prediction 

Trajectory prediction is challenging because the intentions of agents are uncertain. Intuitively, the road structure im-poses certain restrictions on traffic participants’ intentions, as cars are typically expected to drive within the lanes and not stray off the road. Therefore, researchers usually use HD maps to enhance trajectory prediction, as they contain precise information about road structures, such as lanes, pedestrian crossings, and traffic lights. Currently, there are two methods for using HD maps: rasterization and vec-torization. The rasterization method [2, 14, 19, 20] is an earlier solution where researchers render elements of the HD map into a bird’s-eye-view image (with different at-tributes occupying different channels) and then use well-established convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [10, 24] to extract features from the HD map. This method, bene-fiting from the development of CNNs, dominated the field in the early times. However, rasterization has many dis-advantages. First, it is limited by the resolution, which cannot accurately represent the position of map elements, always leading to a loss of precision during the rasteriza-tion process. Second, it is restricted by the receptive field limitations of CNNs, which struggle to capture distant fea-tures well, resulting in lower accuracy of distant trajectory predictions. Third, many locations without map elements still need to be represented numerically, which is redundant and inefficient. To address the issues with rasterization, re-searchers proposed vectorized representation [7, 38, 39]. In vectorized representation, each element of the HD map is represented as a vector (including its position coordinates, category attributes, etc.), and then GNNs or Transformers are used to compute and extract features from the vectors directly. Due to the absence of information loss caused by rasterization and the limitations of the receptive field, vec-torized representations achieve much better results than ras-terized representations in various benchmarks [1, 3, 6, 29]. To fully exploit the potential of SD maps in trajectory pre-diction, we also adopt the vectorized representation.

HD maps provide high-precision and detailed road informa-tion to support precise perception, localization, and plan-ning for autonomous driving systems. However, due to the high cost of HD maps, many researchers are exploring how to use SD maps to advance the development of autonomous driving. ## 3. Methodology 

3.1. Overview 

As shown in Figure 2, SATP is a framework that directly integrates SD map information into HD map-based trajec-tory prediction models. In SATP, SD map information is independently encoded and extracted outside the baseline model and then fused with HD information before the pre-diction head. This SD-HD fusion strategy allows SATP to be easily applied to different types of trajectory prediction models without modifying their internal modules. To en-sure the consistency between SD and HD features, SATP applies model downscaling to create modules for SD map encoding and SD feature extraction. The proposed Align-Net predicts a relative pose between the SD and HD maps and better aligns these two types of maps, which is impor-tant for effectively utilizing SD maps. 

3.2. Preparation of SD Maps 

OpenStreetMap 2 [9] provides global SD maps for free, and we use it as our source of SD maps. For each scenario in the nuScenes [1] and Argoverse2 [29] datasets, we extract the corresponding SD map as follows: 1. Ego-vehicle positioning : We first obtain the latitude and longitude of the ego vehicle based on the original latitude and longitude of the city provided by the dataset and the ground-truth relative pose of the ego vehicle. 2. SD map retrieval : Using the ego vehicle’s pose, we re-trieve the SD map within a certain range centered on the ego vehicle (the specific range is consistent with the HD map range of nuScenes and Argoverse2) 3. Road skeleton extraction : The original SD maps con-tain elements of many attributes, including roads and buildings. For autonomous driving purposes, we ex-tract multilinestring elements that contain the highway 

field, such as highway:primary . These elements repre-sent roads that vehicles can travel on. We then perform equidistant sampling at the 1-meter intervals on these vector elements to accurately capture their shapes. Moreover, it is worth mentioning that the pose of each scene’s ego vehicle obtained by the method above is very accurate, as the dataset provides precise GPS coordinates of the city’s origin, and the relative pose of the ego vehi-cle is highly accurate after complex offline post-processing. However, in practical application, real-time positioning technology often involves larger errors. Since SATP is aimed at real-world autonomous driving, we add random Gaussian noise to the ego vehicle’s pose during the SD map retrieval to simulate positioning errors in real scenarios. 

3.3. SD-HD Fusion 

Our approach to leveraging SD maps is rooted in the fol-lowing principle: SD maps should be integrated into tra-jectory prediction in the same way that the baseline model utilizes HD maps . Therefore, we need to abstract the framework for utilizing HD maps across different base-line models and apply this framework to SD map usage. As illustrated in Figure 3(a), we observe that most recent trajectory prediction models [7, 23, 25, 28, 33, 36, 38, 39] commonly employ a two-step approach when incorporating HD maps: HD map encoding and HD feature extraction. 1. HD Map Encoding: Since HD map elements are gener-ally polylines with specific traffic attributes, they must first be encoded into the feature space to interact with agent motion features and support trajectory predictions effectively. 3.4. AlignNet 

The cropped local SD map from OpenStreetMap based on real-time vehicle positioning results generally has misalign-ment with the corresponding HD map, whether it is an on-line HD map estimated in the ego vehicle’s coordinate sys-tem or an offline constructed ground-truth HD map. As il-lustrated in Figure 4, there are two reasons: (1) The poor precision of SD maps leads to misalignment with the cen-terlines of roads. (2) The vehicle’s positioning results based on GPS/IMU or other technologies have inherent errors. Di-rectly using the misaligned SD maps increases the learning difficulty for the trajectory prediction model due to con-flicting information between misaligned SD and HD maps. Therefore, we design AlignNet to align these two kinds of maps, aiming at improving the effectiveness of SD maps. AlignNet extracts structural features from both the HD map and the SD map, then concatenates these features to predict the translation t and rotation angle θ of the SD map relative to the HD map, and finally applies (θ, t ) to transform the SD map for alignment with the HD map. In AlignNet, we first sample sparse and key points from the input HD and SD maps to preserve maps’ structures but decrease the computational cost of the point transformer. Specifically, for each vector element in the map, we sam-ple at a 5-meter interval and preserve those corner points. Then, a shared light-weighted 2D Point Transformer, which is a variation of the Point Transformer [37], is used as the map encoder. The 2D Point Transformer embeds the coor-dinates of each point as initial features, then performs self-vector attention [35] within its K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) neighborhood to extract local structural features, followed by local max pooling. After multiple iterations of feature extraction and local max pooling, global average pooling is used to obtain the global features. The structural features of HD and SD maps are concatenated, and then an MLP is used to predict the rotation angle θ and transformation t

of the SD map relative to the HD map. Finally, we compute rotation matrix R =

Metrics For trajectory prediction, we follow the offi-cial metrics of Argoverse2, which include minF DE 6,

minADE 6, M R 6. These metrics are calculated based on the best one out of 6 predicted trajectories. FDE refers to the L2 distance between the predicted trajectory and the ground-truth trajectory at the last frame, while ADE is the average L2 error across all predicted time steps. MR (Miss Rate) refers to the proportion of predicted trajectories with an FDE larger than 2 meters. In our tables, we just use ADE, FDE, and MR to represent minF DE 6, minADE 6,and M R 6 for simplicity. For end-to-end autonomous driv-ing, we follow [12] and use the L2 displacement error and collision rate as metrics. L2 displacement error measures the average L2 distance between the planning trajectory and the ground truth. The collision rate represents the frequency of collisions with other agents using the planning trajectory. 

Model Training First, we train the latest online HD map construction models MapTRv2 [16] and HRMapNet [34] on the nuScenes dataset and perform inference with the mapping models to generate the online HD maps for the trajectory prediction scenarios. Then, we train the trajec-tory prediction models with SATP, using the generated on-line HD maps without inference with HD map construction models. All training configurations for the online HD map-ping models and trajectory prediction models are consistent with their official implementations. All trajectory predic-tion models are trained and tested on a single V100 GPU. 

4.1. Performance on nuScenes 

Quantitative Results Table 1 shows the experimental re-sults of deploying SATP on the three latest trajectory pre-diction models, utilizing the state-of-the-art MapTRv2 and HRMapNet for online HD map construction. First, Table 1 demonstrates that STAP significantly enhances the per-formance of these online HD map-based baseline models by integrating SD maps. This is reasonable because per-ception errors in online HD maps can lead to an imprecise understanding of the road environment, and SD maps can correct these errors to some degree. Second, we combine SATP with the ground-truth HD map and find that it can still bring stable improvements to baseline models. The reason is that SD maps contain only the concise and key information of road environment, which could alleviate the feature redundancy problem and improve the training effi-ciency of baseline models. Overall, across the combina-tions of different map inputs and baseline models, SATP brings an average improvement of 9%, 13%, and 17% in terms of ADE, FDE, and MR, respectively. These results demonstrate the value of SATP in real-world autonomous driving applications: (1) When only defective online HD maps are available, SATP can significantly improve the per-formance of HD map-based trajectory prediction models, achieving results comparable to those using perfect ground-truth HD maps. (2) SATP can also play an important role when offline ground-truth HD maps are available in some areas or when significant advancements are made in the fu-ture online HD map construction technology. The method proposed in [8] enhances the performance of online HD Map-based trajectory prediction models by predicting un-certainty for online HD Maps. We combine it with SATP, and experiment results indicate that these two approaches can jointly improve the performance of trajectory predic-tion models based on online HD Maps. 

Qualitative Results In Figure 5, we draw the results of HiVT on the nuScenes dataset with different map inputs. First, it illustrates that SATP effectively utilizes SD maps, achieving predictions superior to those using only online HD maps and comparable to those using precise ground-truth HD maps. This highlights SATP’s value in most real-world scenarios, where ground-truth HD maps are difficult to obtain. Additionally, Figure 5 shows that our AlignNet could align SD maps with HD maps to some extent.

4.6. SD-Enhanced Online HD Map Comparison 

As SD maps can be used to enhance the construction of on-line HD maps, an intuitive idea is to use SD map-enhanced online HD maps to improve trajectory prediction perfor-mance. To explore the differences between this method and SATP, we incorporate the SD map fusion module from P-MapNet[13] into MapTRv2 for experiments. As shown in Table 6, adding SD maps improves the HD map construc-tion quality of MapTRv2, which slightly enhances HiVT, though not as significantly as SATP. We also combine SATP with MapTRv2-PMapNet, and SATP still leads to better trajectory prediction results. We believe that reasons are twofold: (1) Using SD maps to enhance HD map construc-tion, rather than directly for trajectory prediction, results in greater information loss. (2) Although SD map-enhanced online HD maps have fewer perception errors, this way can-not address the feature redundancy of HD maps. 

## 5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose SATP, a framework that integrates SD maps into HD map-based trajectory prediction. This addresses the performance degradation of HD map-only trajectory prediction caused by perception errors and feature redundancy in online HD maps in real-world autonomous driving systems. Our experiments show that SATP yields up to 25% improvement for various representative trajectory prediction models in real-world scenarios while keeping the increase in parameter size and inference time within 10%, which suggests the practical value of SATP for autonomous driving applications. While SATP demonstrates promising results for augmenting HD map-based trajectory prediction, some limitations remain. For instance, we do not account for errors caused by delayed updates of SD maps.
